# Auto-Deleveraging

ADL is the backstop for the backstop: when the vault's inherited liquidation inventory threatens losses the insurance fund cannot absorb, the protocol force-closes the *most profitable, most leveraged counterparties* against that inventory — the standard perp-exchange socialization mechanism, implemented as a permissionless Dutch auction.

## Trigger & freeze

After every matching transaction, liquidation, unload, withdrawal, price refresh, and ADL step, the pair computes the vault's **expected loss** (what closing the inherited inventory at the mark would cost beyond what backs it — the collateral seized with it plus the [ledger's arrears](liquidations.md) to the fund, which settlement collects). When it exceeds the fund's **free capital above its reserve** — the reserve is 10% of the [insurance target](insurance.md) on open interest, kept for the losses no bound can refuse, and sized on the lower of live open interest and its lowest hourly reading over the past day, so positions opened to inflate it cannot bring the trigger forward (see [Insurance Fund](insurance.md)) — ADL goes pending:

- All trading and order creation freeze; position-holders' withdrawals freeze (flat users may still exit — their cash can't affect scores or margin). Insurance deposits are refused too: a batch that closes winners at the estates' bankruptcy price moves part of the inventory's loss from the fund onto them, and that recovery belongs to the LPs the window keeps from redeeming. Capital arriving mid-auction would take a share of it, and would shrink the batch as well — a winner could deposit its own haircut away. The refusal starts at the trigger, not at the latch: a deposit whose own price would open the window is refused as well, so nobody can carry the crossing price in ahead of the health check.
- The trigger price and funding index are snapshotted — the whole auction ranks against this frozen book, so a keeper's off-chain sort and on-chain execution agree; the target side is the *opposite* of the vault's inventory (the winners).
- Hysteresis: pending state only clears once that capital could finish the *remaining* inventory at the edge of the unload band — min(5%, MMR) from the oracle, with MMR the lower of the current rate and the lowest rate recorded over the past day, the hour in progress included, the same figure each batch closes to — so the walk that resumes can sell the rest inside the band without reopening the window, and the boundary can't flap on a tick.
- The unload walk spends only the fund's headroom above that reserve, so an unload can never open a window on its own — and a walk that starts past the trigger takes only fills at or better than the estates' fund-neutral price, which cost the fund nothing, leaving the rest to the auction. Insurance redemptions are held tighter: a paying call pays at most the fund's free capital less the reserve and the cost of finishing the inventory, valued together at whichever edge of the band makes their sum larger, with the band at the current maintenance rate, never narrower than the one a window's release is judged on, and a larger claim only that far. An LP exit can then neither open a window nor spend the reserve, and no move of the mark inside the band opens one after it unless the move also raises the fund target (see [Insurance Fund](insurance.md)). Once a window is open, matching is frozen along with everything else, so until it clears `executeAdl` is the only path that closes inventory against counterparties; the one other path that retires inventory is an opposing liquidation whose netting the fund can afford. See [Liquidations](liquidations.md).
- A hard deadline: pending for **24 hours** without resolution → the pair [winds down](termination.md) at the live price (the settlement price is fixed on the spot and the settlement window opens; anyone finalizes once every position is settled). ADL cannot become a limbo.
- Both the auction and that deadline require a price the oracle currently attests — the stored-price fallback is refused on either. On a pair whose venue is shut, the two are blocked together and would otherwise unblock in the same block at reopen, racing over a pair the auction might still save. So the deadline is not the clock alone: the first price stored past the 24 hours arms it **30 minutes** ahead, and only then may anyone terminate. The window is set once and never extended — a per-price refresh would be a snooze button, since the [refresh bounty](fees.md) pays keepers to store a price every ~5 minutes while the vault holds inventory worth $1,250 or more, as it typically does through a window — and it is cleared when the auction resolves, so a later window on the same pair starts with its own full runway.
- If the vault's inventory flips to the other side mid-auction — an opposing liquidation the fund could afford netted the old aggregate away, or the deferred liquidations below were seized once it was gone — the target side is re-pointed, the price/funding snapshot re-taken and the score threshold restarted at 25× — but the 24-hour clock is deliberately *not* reset, so repeated flips can never stall termination. A flip can also happen inside a batch, when its own deferred pass nets past the inventory or empties it and seeds a new one; the batch then skips its inventory leg, because the `winners` it was given now sit on the inventory's side, and closing them against it would take that exposure out of one side of the book twice and leave the other side with nothing opposite it. The listed accounts the pass reaches after the flip sit on the inventory's side, and they merge into it, as `liquidate()` would merge them. The deferred resolutions stand, the health check after the batch re-points the window, and the next batch ranks the new cohort.
- A window also opens, and stays open, while any **deferred liquidation** is under water: an insolvent position opposite the inventory that `liquidate()` left open because netting it would have taken the fund below its reserve (see [Liquidations](liquidations.md)). Its loss beyond its collateral is exposure no earmark backs, and unlike the inventory leg no amount of fund capital covers it in place — the excess is a property of the trader's bucket, and the gate reads it from there. Capital still resolves it, by a different route: the netting is re-tested on every `liquidate()` that names the account and on every auction pass, so a fund that recovers enough to afford the entry gap absorbs the position into the inventory with no deleveraging at all. Failing that the auction closes the account's own counterparties against it, and failing that too — no cohort covers it — the auction closes enough of the inventory's own counterparties to give the fund the means to absorb it, at the mark wherever the inventory's own exposure does not need the close (see the batch below). An account that turns solvent again — a reversal, a deposit — is simply delisted, and so is one the inventory has flipped onto its own side: a netting the fund could afford can move the inventory to a listed account's side, after which no batch can close that account through the window, while any `liquidate()` that names it seizes it into the inventory. The gate does not count such an account. Counted only while the vault holds inventory: once that is gone the next liquidation seizes the deferred positions and they become inventory in their own right. Any health check that leaves no window open clears the whole list, whether it resolves one — by a batch or otherwise — or finds none to open: no window means no listed account still carries uncovered loss while the fund covers the inventory it sits opposite, which makes netting any of them affordable, or there is no inventory left to net one against, so any that stay open are ordinary liquidation candidates again. A single `liquidate()` can reach that state with an account it has just listed, because its inline unload runs between the deferral and the health check and can sell the inventory the account was deferred against. The one entry that outlives a health check with no window stored is a deferral on the stale-price fallback: a stale mark never opens a window, so the entry waits for the fresh verdict that does.

**When the fund is at or below its reserve**, the capital above it is zero and the gate collapses to its strictest form: any paper loss on inherited inventory opens a window on the next fresh verdict, each batch is sized to close the whole inventory unless the reserve its own closes release can carry the rest, and the window clears only once the inventory is gone or sits above water by more than the band. The vault meanwhile sells only at its neutral price or better, so nothing is realized at the fund's expense, and winners carry every loss beyond the seizures while the taker insurance fee runs near the top of its deficit curve (about 40× its base rate at a tenth of target coverage, capped at 50 bp) and LP withdrawals stay blocked. Every dollar of fees or deposits above the reserve restores a dollar of headroom and moves the trigger off zero — there is no memory and no separate threshold at the reserve line. [Share pricing](insurance.md) reads the same figure: a deposit or redemption deducts the inventory's unrealized loss only as far as the capital above the reserve covers it, because beyond that the auction charges the remainder to winners rather than to the fund. No share changes hands past that point: a redemption's own health check refuses there, and so does a deposit, whose capital would shrink or cancel the auction that charge rests on. In this regime, then, LP flows wait: a redemption while the vault holds any inherited inventory, since it pays only from capital above the reserve and the finish, valued together at the band's edge, and there is none, and a deposit while that inventory sits under water.

## The auction

Anyone may call `executeAdl(winners[], deferredWinners[], priceUpdate)` with up to 25 candidates per list, each sorted by descending **ADL score**, ties by ascending address. The order is checked on the entries the batch admits until they cover the inventory (for `deferredWinners`, the listed accounts' combined size), and an admitted entry out of order there, a repeat included, reverts. Entries past that point are not checked: the close loop reaches them only when an admitted winner settles at a loss at the batch's price and is skipped, and a repeat among them closes nothing extra (the inventory leg finds the account already closed or the batch complete, and the deferred leg reverts). This gives a keeper no choice it lacks anyway, since any descending subset is a valid submission:

```
adlScore = positionPnL(at snapshot price) / collateral
```

— profit-to-collateral, so a 10× levered winner outranks a 2× one at equal profit. Collateral for scoring **excludes deposits made during the current ADL window** (epoch-tagged at deposit time): topping up mid-auction protects your margin but cannot re-rank you out of the queue. This applies to the side the window targets, whose score is pinned to the snapshot; the inventory's own side, when it ranks as the deferred cohort below, ranks on its full collateral. A withdrawn-to-zero winner scores effectively infinite — pure-profit claims stand first in line.

Eligibility decays like a Dutch auction: the score threshold starts at **25×** and decays quadratically to ~0 over **10 minutes**. The worst offenders are executable immediately; by minute ten, any profitable counterparty is.

The decay measures time the auction could actually run. ADL needs a price the oracle currently attests, so across a closure the curve would otherwise descend against attempts nobody was able to make — and the first keeper at the reopen would find the gate wide open, free to deleverage any profitable account instead of the worst-ranked ones the curve exists to reach first. A stored price that ends a gap of at least **1 hour** — the same discontinuity threshold the [reopen-gap leg](margin.md#the-stress-move) of the pair's stress move learns from, set so the refresh bounty's cadence separates a shut venue from ordinary call sparsity — therefore restarts the decay at 25×. Restarting forfeits whatever the auction had already spent, which is at most the ten minutes; the trade is a clock that is a single instant rather than an accumulated one. The 24-hour termination clock is separate and never rewinds, so a pair that keeps failing to deleverage still resolves.

### Two cohorts, one batch

A batch works two independent problems, each against its own counterparties, and a keeper submits whichever cohorts are needed. `winners` is the side *opposite* the vault's inventory, closed against the inventory as described below. `deferredWinners` is the inventory's *own* side, closed against the deferred liquidations: each deferred account is seized inside the batch, its counterparties are settled against it at its own bankruptcy price (or at the mark while the account still holds equity, the rule [Settlement](#settlement) applies to the inventory), and nothing of it outlives the transaction. The bankruptcy price is where the seized collateral covers the loss exactly (at the mark it covers more), and no holding window has accrued any funding carry, so this leg costs the fund nothing. A deferred account is taken whole or not at all — only when the cohort can absorb its whole position **at that settlement price**, measured before anything is seized — so nothing seized here is ever left half-closed as a second inventory. That the test is the settleable size rather than the eligible one is load-bearing, because the two prices disagree: a winner whose entry sits between the live mark and the settlement price is profitable at the mark and under water at the settlement, and the close skips it rather than force a market-profitable trader into a realized loss. Counting it would seize an account with nothing to close against and drop the estate into the netting deferral exists to avoid — which any caller could arrange, since the winner list is theirs to choose. What a close does leave behind, reachable only when one of its own slices books a `deficit` and stops the batch, is absorbed into the inventory with the netting's cap as the backstop. A remainder with no deficit behind it means the test counted size the close could not consume, and the batch reverts rather than net it; the ordering rule, which admits no account twice, keeps an honest submission from getting there.

This cohort ranks at the **live price**, not the window's frozen snapshot — only the Dutch threshold it must clear is the window's. A deferred account enters the window *after* that snapshot was taken, at a price that made it bankrupt, so the profit its counterparties hold exists at the live price and not necessarily at the snapshot: an inventory seized on the way down freezes a snapshot at which the longs a later rise bankrupted shorts against had no profit at all, and ranking them there would score the whole cohort ineligible. It ranks on **live collateral** too: window deposits are not subtracted from this cohort. The subtraction pins the target side's score to the snapshot its price is frozen at, and this cohort has no such snapshot; its deposits were made while its side was the one being liquidated, to defend against that, not against a ranking it was not yet subject to — the same reason a re-target voids the tags of the side it brings into range. A top-up therefore lowers an account's place in this cohort, as posting more margin lowers its leverage. The live price is the one the call resolves and stores (`executeAdl` refuses a stale mark), so the ranking is fixed within a call but not across calls the way the snapshot is across a window. Nor is it the price the keeper submits: the pair reads Pyth's on-chain cache before the keeper's update, so when a newer tick reaches the cache first — anyone can write it — the ranking moves to that tick. `BazaarPairLens.getAdlScore` follows the same split: it scores `winners` at the snapshot and this cohort at the pair's last stored price with the live funding index, which is the on-chain key whenever the transaction stores nothing newer. A keeper therefore scores this cohort at the price its transaction will store — simplest as one `eth_call` that simulates `refreshPrice(priceUpdate)` and then reads `getAdlScore` for each candidate — and, when a newer tick overtakes it and the batch reverts `AdlLib__NotDescendingAdlScoreOrder`, re-scores and resubmits. The revert costs gas only; the `winners` leg does not depend on it. Once the inventory is empty, any deferred accounts still open and insolvent are seized on the spot and become the inventory, and the health check that follows re-targets the window at their counterparties.

Netting the two sides against each other at the oracle would charge the fund a loss that belongs to the deferred accounts' winners, which is exactly the case deferral exists to avoid. The two resolutions interleave on their own, though: every close retires open interest, the reserve is a fraction of open interest, so a batch frees capital as it runs and a netting that was unaffordable when it began can become affordable partway through — the accounts still listed are then absorbed that way instead of auctioned. `auxState().deferredCount` reports how many are listed and `deferredLiquidations(i)` names them; `BazaarPairLens.getAdlScore` scores an account on the inventory's side only while some are listed.

An account the deferred pass leaves listed **past its collateral** — no cohort covers it whole, and the fund cannot afford its netting while keeping its reserve and the whole remaining inventory's bankruptcy-priced close backed — is resolved by the inventory leg, which is then sized for it (see the batch size below). Such an account is what holds the window open; one within its collateral holds nothing open and simply waits for the release that clears the list, or for a `liquidate()` that names it. Nothing else can take an account past its collateral: it sits opposite the inventory and the vault holds one aggregate, so seizing it *is* the netting, and the window it holds open halts every unload. What moves the fund's means is closing inventory: each unit closed settles its slice against its earmark (a close at a mark past the bankruptcy price costs the fund the distance too), takes its share off what the fund has to hold for the rest, and, once open interest is down to the reading the reserve is sized on, frees that unit's share of the reserve. The leg closes enough of its winners for those nettings to fit — none at all when the fund can carry what the nettings leave as it stands, since they retire inventory themselves — the deferred pass runs once more after it with no cohort and takes only those accounts, and the fund absorbs them and pays what it can afford — exactly what the `liquidate()` that deferred them would have done had the fund held the cash then. The loss stays with the fund, not with the inventory's winners. Only the units the batch finds the fund cannot carry close at the inventory leg's settlement price (see [Settlement](#settlement)); the units the leg closes past them, only so the nettings fit, close at the mark, and the fund pays their distance to the settlement price. Closing those at the bankruptcy price would instead haircut the inventory's winners to pay a loss the deferred accounts owe their own counterparties, who sit on the inventory's side. A keeper that submits both lists therefore gets the cheaper resolution automatically: a cohort that covers an account closes it at no cost to the fund and earns the executor 0.1% of what it averted, before the inventory leg is sized; a submission without the covering cohort reaches the netting even where one existed, and puts the loss on the fund within what it can afford.

## Settlement

Winners close at the **average bankruptcy price of the dead estates** — the price at which the vault's inventory realizes exactly the collateral it seized, so the close is neutral to the fund whatever the winner's own entry was — or at the **mark**, whenever the mark sits on the fund's favorable side of that price. That price applies to the inventory the batch finds the fund cannot carry; a batch that closes more, so the fund can absorb deferred accounts, closes the rest at the mark (see the batch size below). The bankruptcy price is the estates' summed bankruptcy notional over their size, rounded toward the fund: up when the vault is selling inherited longs, down when it is buying back inherited shorts. A window can outlive a recovery past the bankruptcy price (it releases only once the fund could finish the remainder at the band edge), and `executeAdl` is permissionless with a caller-chosen winner list, so settling there at the bankruptcy price would let a winner deleverage themselves better than market at the fund's expense. Taking the mark keeps the close never better than market for the winner and hands the recovery to the fund:

- Winners whose PnL at that price would be *negative* are skipped (never worse than break-even).
- Oversized winners are partially closed pro-rata.
- The winner realizes their **full PnL at that price** into their bucket, exactly as a maker filled by a vault unload does. The part of that PnL between the winner's entry and the estate's entry was already settled by the winner's earlier counterparties, so it is backed by the deposits ledger, not by the fund.
- The inventory slice settles through insurance against the liquidatees' entry, **price and funding legs together**, as on an unload fill. At the bankruptcy price the two legs net to the seizure (already in the fund) plus the funding accrued while the vault held the inventory, because the bankruptcy price already embeds the estate's pre-liquidation funding; at a better mark the loss is smaller by the distance and the fund keeps the rest of the earmark. The fund can therefore only fall short by that holding-window carry: what it cannot pay is **withheld from the winner's funding income** — never from their price PnL — and reported in the `UserAdld` event. A shortfall beyond that is recorded as `deficit` and [winds the pair down](termination.md). Rounding is the one exception. A slice's shares of the entry basis and the earmark are each rounded on their own, so a close can land a few wei past its earmark with no carry at all; up to **one millionth of a USDC** per close (the smallest amount the pair can pay out) comes off the winner's credit even where they earned no funding, and a remainder that small which the credit cannot absorb is not recorded as `deficit`, because a deficit of any size ends the pair. (A seizure the deposits ledger could only partly pay is not a shortfall: the unpaid part is the ledger's arrears to the fund, and a slice's settlement nets its share of them.) Each slice is funded from its own seizure earmark, the arrears and the fund's free capital, never from the earmark of inventory still open, so within a batch the highest-scored winners are paid in full and any withholding lands on the later ones. It spends them in this order: its own earmark first, then the arrears, then free capital. A slice whose earmark and share of the arrears cover its loss therefore leaves the inventory it does not close at least as backed per unit as before, and a deferred account's close against its cohort, which loses no more than the account's seizure and unpaid collateral, reaches nothing the vault's own inventory relies on beyond rounding. Netting the arrears first would leave the inventory reading worse to the netting gate and to the window test, which both count the arrears in full, while the released earmark sat in free capital below the reserve, where neither counts it.

A batch closes **only as much inventory as restores the fund**, never the whole aggregate because one winner happened to be large enough to cover it. When the deferred pass has left accounts listed past their collateral, "restores the fund" means more: the batch is sized to the figure after which the fund can net every such account and still keep what the inventory that remains needs, the executor's rewards paid, so that one batch ends the window rather than leaving the fund at its reserve against the remainder. What that inventory needs is the larger of two figures, each beyond its earmark: what finishing it at the band edge would cost, and what closing it at the estates' bankruptcy price would cost — the collateral the deposits ledger could not hand over at seizure and the net funding the vault paid holding it — which the [netting gate](liquidations.md) holds back so that a later bankruptcy-priced close of it books no deficit. The first is the larger when the band edge lies past the bankruptcy price, on the side where the inventory loses, the second when the edge lies short of it, where the first can be nothing while the second is not; sized on the first alone, the batch would close nothing while the gate refused every netting, batch after batch, until the timeout. This netting figure stands in for the inventory's own restoring figure (below) rather than competing with it: the nettings take inventory off the fund too, so it can be the smaller of the two, and closing to the larger would haircut winners for units the nettings retire. That holds down to zero: when the fund can net the accounts as it stands, the batch closes nothing and nets them, whether or not any winner is eligible. The nettings after the leg keep backed only what remains once all of them are made, and only when the leg closed the full figure; a leg that closed less — too few eligible winners, a winner skipped at the price it would close at, or a close stopped by a deficit — leaves each netting keeping the whole remaining inventory backed, as the pass before the leg does; otherwise the first nettings could spend backing the inventory still needs once a later one is refused. Only the units the inventory's own figure calls for settle at the settlement price; the rest of that size settles at the mark, so it is reckoned on what a unit closed at the mark frees — less, when the mark is past the bankruptcy price, by the distance between the two. Those closes run only once the settlement-priced closes are complete; a batch whose settlement-priced closes stop short — a winner at a loss at the bankruptcy price is skipped — closes no more and leaves the rest to the next batch. They settle at the mark, and the fund can always pay their distance to the settlement price: the settlement-priced closes leave it able to finish what remains at the band edge, which lies further from the settlement price than the mark does, so the capital that carries the remainder covers that distance on any part of it, with the reserve kept. That size holds while the inventory left after the close still covers the listed accounts; where it would not — the nettings would consume the inventory and flip the remainder onto their side, with the fund having paid for inventory it does not keep — the whole inventory goes instead, nothing is spent on nettings, and the accounts are seized as the new inventory with nothing to net against. When the fund already carried the inventory as the batch began — the deferred accounts alone holding the window open — and the inventory is under water, this is where the batch ends, unless the reserve each unit closed at the mark releases exceeds what it costs the fund beyond the backing the netting gate already holds for that unit: the distance between the mark and the bankruptcy price, with no reserve released while live open interest sits above its trailing reading. (The collateral the ledger never paid and the funding the vault paid holding the unit leave the fund with the close, but come off what the remaining inventory has to keep in equal measure.) The accounts were left unnetted because their cost exceeds the fund's capital above the reserve and the arrears it may spend, and every close at the mark lowers that capital by its cost, less the reserve it releases. The fund then pays the inventory's loss at the mark, and whatever it cannot carry of the deferred accounts goes to their own counterparties once the window re-targets. Otherwise the size is computed up front from the gap between what finishing the inventory at the band edge would cost beyond its earmark and the fund's free capital above its reserve plus the ledger's arrears to it: each unit closed removes its share of that cost and hands the fund what its settlement is worth — at the bankruptcy price, the earmark less the collateral the ledger never paid and the funding accrued while the vault held the unit, the funding the estates paid or received before liquidation being already inside that price — less what it pays the executor (below), so every leg sets the figure. A closed unit also takes its share of the reserve off, since the reserve follows open interest — but only once the close has brought live open interest down to the trailing reading the reserve is sized on; while live open interest sits above that reading, closing units releases nothing and the batch is sized without it. Whatever the same batch already earned closing the deferred cohort is paid out of that capital too, so it counts toward the distance the inventory leg has to cover. Whatever the batch does not need stays with the winner. Only with a zero band, where the settlement price can be the band edge itself and a close takes at least as much off the capital as off the finish cost, does no size restore health; the auction then runs to its eligible size and the timeout or a recorded `deficit` resolves it. No pair's band is zero, since the maintenance rate it derives from never falls below 2%.

For the inventory its own exposure needs closed, the executor earns **2 bp of its notional, valued at the mark**, or **0.1% of the bad debt the close averts** where that is larger, which it is only once the mark lies more than a fifth of itself past the bankruptcy price. That is the units the restoring figure calls for, at whichever price they settle, up to what the fund could not carry of the inventory as the batch began, less the size of every deferred account the batch absorbs, whether it nets the account or makes it the new inventory. Those units take the inventory's exposure off the fund, as an unload's sale does, while the window halts unloads, so they are paid the rate an unload is paid, at the mark as at the bankruptcy price. The other units the batch closes, there only so the fund can absorb deferred accounts, earn nothing beyond those accounts' seizure rewards: a cohort that covers an account spares the fund its loss, and paying the inventory route more would pay a keeper for leaving the cohort out. Counting the paid units this way, every account the batch absorbs instead of closing it against its cohort lowers the leg's pay by its own size. The count is the batch's own: an account netted by an earlier call, a `liquidate()` or a batch with no winners, is not counted against a later batch's leg. For every deferred account it seized in the batch, the executor earns max($0.05, 2 bp of the notional, valued at the mark, that the batch retires with it: the size its counterparties closed, or the overlap it netted against the inventory), plus 0.1% of the bad debt a close against the account's counterparties averts. An account the batch moves into the inventory instead — seized once the inventory is gone, or merged into an inventory a larger netting just moved onto its side — is exposure moved onto the fund and earns the $0.05 alone, as a [liquidation](liquidations.md) seizing it into the inventory would, and the part of a netted account larger than the inventory adds nothing either; whoever later retires that exposure is paid for it. The $0.05 is limited to the account's own equity when its position is worth less than $5, as for liquidations. All of it is debited together from the insurance fund's free capital (the earmark left against unclosed inventory never pays it) and accrued to its claimable balance (`claimFees()`) — so the reward is bounded by what the exposure actually required. The batch is sized with the reward counted, so paying it never leaves the fund short of releasing the window.
